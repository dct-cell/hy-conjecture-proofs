# A counterexample for Problem 32(b)

We take the residue field to be $k=\mathbf F_2$.  Put
$$
V=k[[t]],\qquad K=k((t)),
$$
and define
$$
D=k[[t^4,t^5,t^{11}]]\subseteq V.
$$
More precisely, $D$ is the image of $k[[X,Y,Z]]$ under
$X\mapsto t^4,\ Y\mapsto t^5,\ Z\mapsto t^{11}$.  Since all weights are positive, this formal evaluation is well defined.  From the numerical semigroup generated by $4,5,11$, we have
$$
D=k\oplus kt^4\oplus kt^5\oplus t^8V. \tag{1}
$$
Indeed, the image is clearly contained in the right-hand side.  Conversely, an element of the right-hand side can be written as the image of
$$
a_0+a_4X+a_5Y+X^2A(X)+XYB(X)+Y^2C(X)+ZH(X),
$$
where
$$
A(X)=\sum_{q\ge0}a_{8+4q}X^q,\quad
B(X)=\sum_{q\ge0}a_{9+4q}X^q,\quad
C(X)=\sum_{q\ge0}a_{10+4q}X^q,\quad
H(X)=\sum_{q\ge0}a_{11+4q}X^q.
$$

All colons and $v$-operations in the next three sections are taken with respect to $D$.

## 1. The basic properties of $D$

The complete power-series theorem [1, Lemma 10.31.2] says that if $R$ is Noetherian, then so is $R[[X_1,\dots,X_n]]$.  Thus $k[[X,Y,Z]]$ is Noetherian, and $D$, being a homomorphic image, is Noetherian.  Every Noetherian ring has the ascending chain condition on all ideals.  Consequently every ascending chain of nonzero integral divisorial ideals of $D$ stabilizes; hence $D$ is Mori.

We next identify the fraction field.  Since
$$
t=\frac{t^5}{t^4}\in\operatorname{Frac}(D),
\tag{2}
$$
and, by (1),
$$
V=D+kt+kt^2+kt^3+kt^6+kt^7,
\tag{3}
$$
we have $V\subseteq\operatorname{Frac}(D)$.  As $\operatorname{Frac}(V)=k((t))$, it follows that
$$
\operatorname{Frac}(D)=K=k((t)).
$$
Equation (3) also shows that $V$ is a finite $D$-module.  A finite ring extension is integral, so $V$ is integral over $D$.  The ring $V=k[[t]]$ is a DVR and hence integrally closed; therefore $V$ is the integral closure of $D$.

We use the integral-extension theorem [2, Lemma 10.112.4] in the following precise form: if $A\subseteq B$ is an integral extension of domains, then lying over, going up, and incomparability give
$$
\dim A=\dim B.
$$
Here $D\subseteq V$ is integral and $\dim V=1$; hence
$$
\dim D=1.
$$

Finally, (1) immediately gives the conductor
$$
F:=(D:V)=t^8V. \tag{4}
$$
Indeed $t^8V\subseteq D$, while $t^rV\not\subseteq D$ for $0\le r<8$, and any $z$ with $zV\subseteq D$ must already belong to $V$ and have least $t$-order at least $8$.  From (4),
$$
(D:F)=(D:t^8V)=V: \tag{5}
$$
if $zF\subseteq D$, then $zt^8V\subseteq D$, so
$zt^8\in(D:V)=F=t^8V$, and cancellation gives $z\in V$; the reverse inclusion is immediate.

## 2. Reduction of all ideals to normalized ideals

For $0\ne c\in K$ and a nonzero fractional ideal $J$, the definition gives
$$
(D:cJ)=c^{-1}(D:J),\qquad (cJ)^{v}=cJ^{v}. \tag{6}
$$
Likewise, divisoriality and the two-generator property are unchanged by multiplication by $c$.

Let $0\ne J\subseteq D$ be divisorial.  Choose $a\in J$ having the least $t$-adic order among elements of $J$, and set
$$
L=a^{-1}J.
$$
Then
$$
D\subseteq L\subseteq V, \tag{7}
$$
because $1\in L$ and every element of $L$ has nonnegative $t$-order.  Moreover $L$ is divisorial by (6).  Conversely, multiplying any divisorial $L$ as in (7) by a suitable nonzero $a\in K$ gives an integral divisorial ideal.  It therefore suffices to classify the divisorial $L$'s in (7), with $v$ still computed in $D$.

For such an $L$, put
$$
C=(D:L),\qquad W=L/D\subseteq Q:=V/D.
$$
Because $L\subseteq V$ and $1\in L$,
$$
F\subseteq C\subseteq D.
$$
Hence, by (5),
$$
D\subseteq L^v=(D:C)\subseteq(D:F)=V.
$$

Introduce
$$
\mathcal A=V/F,\qquad \Delta=D/F,
$$
and write $\epsilon_i=t^i+F$ for $0\le i<8$.  Thus
$$
\mathcal A=\bigoplus_{i=0}^7k\epsilon_i,\qquad
\Delta=\langle\epsilon_0,\epsilon_4,\epsilon_5\rangle,
$$
and we identify
$$
Q=V/D=\langle\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2,\epsilon_3,\epsilon_6,\epsilon_7\rangle
$$
using these representatives.  Set
$$
x=\epsilon_4,\qquad y=\epsilon_5.
$$
The induced actions of $x$ and $y$ on $Q$ are
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
 & \epsilon_1&\epsilon_2&\epsilon_3&\epsilon_6&\epsilon_7\\ \hline
x&0&\epsilon_6&\epsilon_7&0&0\\
y&\epsilon_6&\epsilon_7&0&0&0 .
\end{array} \tag{8}
$$
(The products $x\epsilon_1=\epsilon_5$ and $y\epsilon_3=\epsilon_8=0$, for example, are read modulo $F$.)

Under the identification $L/F=\Delta\oplus W$, we have
$$
\overline C:=C/F
 =\{\overline z\in\Delta:\overline z\,W\subseteq\Delta\}, \tag{9}
$$
and, because $L^v\subseteq V$,
$$
L^v/F
 =\{\overline r\in\mathcal A:\overline r\,\overline C\subseteq\Delta\}. \tag{10}
$$
In (10), if $\overline C=0$, then $C=F$ and (5) gives $L^v=V$.

## 3. Classification of divisorial ideals

First suppose $W\ne0$.  Write
$$
U=\langle\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2,\epsilon_3\rangle,\qquad
S=\langle\epsilon_6,\epsilon_7\rangle,
$$
so that $Q=U\oplus S$.  By (8), $x$ and $y$ map $U$ into $S$ and annihilate $S$.

Take $\overline z=a_0+a_1x+a_2y\in\overline C$.  If $w\in W$, equation (9), read in $Q$, gives
$$
a_0w+a_1(xw)+a_2(yw)=0. \tag{11}
$$
Projecting to $U$ gives $a_0\,\pi_U(w)=0$ for every $w\in W$.  If $\pi_U(W)\ne0$, then $a_0=0$.  If $\pi_U(W)=0$, then $0\ne W\subseteq S$, and (11) reduces to $a_0w=0$; again $a_0=0$.  Thus
$$
0\ne W\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\overline C\subseteq\langle x,y\rangle.
$$
Since $k=\mathbf F_2$, the vector space $\langle x,y\rangle$ has exactly the five subspaces
$$
0,\ \langle x\rangle,\ \langle y\rangle,\ \langle x+y\rangle,\ \langle x,y\rangle.
$$

It remains to calculate the double colon for these five possibilities, and also for $\overline C=\Delta$ when $W=0$.  Let
$$
r=\sum_{i=0}^7a_i\epsilon_i\in\mathcal A.
$$
Multiplication gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
rx&=a_0\epsilon_4+a_1\epsilon_5+a_2\epsilon_6+a_3\epsilon_7,\\
ry&=a_0\epsilon_5+a_1\epsilon_6+a_2\epsilon_7,\\
r(x+y)&=a_0\epsilon_4+(a_0+a_1)\epsilon_5
 +(a_1+a_2)\epsilon_6+(a_2+a_3)\epsilon_7.
\end{aligned} \tag{12}
$$
Membership in $\Delta$ means that the coefficients of $\epsilon_6,\epsilon_7$ vanish.  Formula (10) therefore yields the following table:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\overline C & L^v/F \\ \hline
0&\mathcal A\\
\langle x,y\rangle&\Delta+\langle\epsilon_6,\epsilon_7\rangle\\
\langle x\rangle&\Delta+\langle\epsilon_1,\epsilon_6,\epsilon_7\rangle\\
\langle y\rangle&\Delta+\langle\epsilon_3,\epsilon_6,\epsilon_7\rangle\\
\langle x+y\rangle&
\Delta+\langle\epsilon_6,\epsilon_7,\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2+\epsilon_3\rangle\\
\Delta&\Delta.
\end{array} \tag{13}
$$

Suppose now that $L$ is divisorial.  If $W=0$, then $L=D$.  If $W\ne0$, (13) shows that $W$ must be the quotient modulo $\Delta$ of one of the five other entries in the right-hand column.  Thus $W$ is among
$$
0,\quad
S,\quad
S+\langle\epsilon_1\rangle,\quad
S+\langle\epsilon_3\rangle,\quad
S+\langle q\rangle,\quad
Q, \tag{14}
$$
where $q=\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2+\epsilon_3$.

Conversely, every subspace in (14) really is stable under the actions (8).  For a subspace $W\subseteq Q$, write $L(W)$ for its full inverse image in $V$; thus $L(W)/D=W$.  The following table gives, for each of the six possibilities, a two-generated submodule
$$
N=L(P)=D+uD\subseteq L(W)
$$
whose colon $\overline C$ is the same as that of $L(W)$:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
W & u & P=N/D & \overline C(N)=\overline C(L(W))\\ \hline
0&0&0&\Delta\\
S&t^6&\langle\epsilon_6\rangle&\langle x,y\rangle\\
S+\langle\epsilon_1\rangle&t&
 \langle\epsilon_1,\epsilon_6\rangle&\langle x\rangle\\
S+\langle\epsilon_3\rangle&t^3&
 \langle\epsilon_3,\epsilon_7\rangle&\langle y\rangle\\
S+\langle q\rangle&t+t^2+t^3&
 \langle q,\epsilon_6+\epsilon_7\rangle&\langle x+y\rangle\\
Q&t^2&
 \langle\epsilon_2,\epsilon_6,\epsilon_7\rangle&0.
\end{array} \tag{15}
$$
The third column follows directly from (1) and (8); for example,
$$
\begin{aligned}
D+tD&=D\oplus kt\oplus kt^6,\\
D+t^3D&=D\oplus kt^3\oplus kt^7,\\
D+(t+t^2+t^3)D&=
D\oplus k(t+t^2+t^3)\oplus k(t^6+t^7).
\end{aligned}
$$
The fourth column also follows from (8).  For instance, on
$\langle\epsilon_1,\epsilon_6\rangle$, $x$ is zero but $y$ is not; on
$\langle q,\epsilon_6+\epsilon_7\rangle$, both $x$ and $y$ send $q$ to
$\epsilon_6+\epsilon_7$, so their sum is the unique nonzero colon element.

For every row, $N$ and $L(W)$ have the same $\overline C$; hence (13) gives
$$
N^v=L(W)\qquad\text{and}\qquad L(W)^v=L(W). \tag{16}
$$
Therefore (14) is exactly the list of possible $W$'s for a divisorial normalized ideal $L$.

Now let $0\ne J\subseteq D$ be any nonzero divisorial ideal.  Choose $a\in J$ of least $t$-order and set $L=a^{-1}J$ as above.  Then $L=L(W)$ for one of the six subspaces in (14).  Let $u$ be the corresponding element in table (15).  Since $P\subseteq W$, both $1$ and $u$ belong to $L(W)$, and (16) gives
$$
J=aL(W)
  =aN^v
  =(aN)^v
  =\bigl(aD+auD\bigr)^v. \tag{17}
$$
The elements $a$ and $au$ belong to $J$, and $a\ne0$ (in the first row one simply takes $u=0$).  Thus every nonzero divisorial integral ideal of $D$ is two-generated with respect to $v_D$.  This verifies the global two-generator hypothesis, not merely the condition for the ideal chosen below.

## 4. A divisorial ideal that is not stable

We now prove that the conclusion fails.  Let
$$
L_1=L\bigl(S+\langle\epsilon_1\rangle\bigr).
$$
By (1),
$$
L_1=D+kt+kt^6+kt^7=k\oplus kt\oplus t^4V. \tag{18}
$$
It is divisorial by (16).  Put
$$
I:=t^4L_1=kt^4\oplus kt^5\oplus t^8V.
$$
This is a nonzero integral ideal of $D$; in fact it is the maximal ideal
$(t^4,t^5,t^{11})D$.  By (6),
$$
I^v=t^4L_1^v=t^4L_1=I,
$$
so $I$ is divisorial.

We compute its endomorphism ring.  Multiplication by a nonzero field element does not change the endomorphism ring:
$$
E(cJ)=(cJ:cJ)=(J:J)=E(J).
$$
Hence $E(I)=E(L_1)$.  Define the overring
$$
B:=k\oplus t^4V.
$$
From (18), one has
$$
B L_1\subseteq L_1,
$$
so $B\subseteq(L_1:L_1)$.  Conversely, if $z\in(L_1:L_1)$, then $z=z\cdot1\in L_1$.  Write
$$
z=c+\alpha t+r,\qquad c,\alpha\in k,\quad r\in t^4V.
$$
Since $t\in L_1$,
$$
zt=ct+\alpha t^2+tr\in L_1.
$$
Here $ct\in L_1$, $tr\in t^5V\subseteq L_1$, but $L_1$ has zero $t^2$-coefficient.  Since there can be no cancellation at degree $2$, $\alpha=0$.  Hence $z\in B$, and
$$
E(I)=E(L_1)=B. \tag{19}
$$

We now compute the ordinary colon in $E(I)=B$.  First,
$$
(B:L_1)=t^4V. \tag{20}
$$
Indeed, if $zL_1\subseteq B$, then $z\in B$ because $1\in L_1$.  Write $z=c+r$ with $c\in k$ and $r\in t^4V$.  Since $t\in L_1$,
$$
zt=ct+rt\in B.
$$
The term $rt$ has order at least $5$, while $B$ has zero $t$-coefficient; hence $c=0$, so $z\in t^4V$.  Conversely, $t^4V L_1\subseteq t^4V\subseteq B$.

For ordinary colons we also have
$$
(B:cY)=c^{-1}(B:Y).
$$
Using $I=t^4L_1$ and (20),
$$
(E(I):I)
=(B:t^4L_1)
=t^{-4}(B:L_1)
=t^{-4}t^4V
=V. \tag{21}
$$
Since $1\in L_1\subseteq V$, one has $L_1V=V$.  Consequently
$$
I(E(I):I)
=t^4L_1V
=t^4V
\subsetneq B
=E(I). \tag{22}
$$
The inclusion is strict because $1\in B$ but $1\notin t^4V$.  Thus $I$ is not invertible as an ideal of $E(I)$; equivalently, $I$ is not stable.

We have therefore constructed a one-dimensional Mori domain $D$ satisfying the two-$v$-generator hypothesis for every nonzero divisorial integral ideal, while the divisorial ideal
$$
I=(t^4,t^5,t^{11})D
$$
is not stable.  This disproves the assertion.
