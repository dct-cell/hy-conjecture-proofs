# Problem 5(a): an explicit counterexample

Let $K=\mathbb F_{3}$, and let
$$
G=\bigoplus_{i\ge 0} C_{2}
$$
be the direct sum of countably many cyclic groups of order $2$.  Write $g_i$ for the generator of the $i$-th copy of $C_2$.  Thus every nonidentity element of $G$ has order $2$.  Put
$$
A:=K[G],\qquad B:=K[X],
$$
where $X$ is an indeterminate, and set
$$
R_1:=A,\qquad R_2:=B.
$$

We shall show that
$$
\operatorname{gl.dim}R_1=\operatorname{gl.dim}R_2=1
$$
but
$$
\operatorname{gl.dim}R_1[G]=1
\quad\text{and}\quad
\operatorname{gl.dim}R_2[G]=2.
$$

Two elementary facts will be used repeatedly.

**Fact 1.** If every ideal of a commutative ring $S$ is projective, then $\operatorname{gl.dim}S\le 1$.

Indeed, let $L$ be a submodule of a free $S$-module $F$.  Well-order a basis $\{e_\alpha\}_{\alpha<\kappa}$ of $F$, and set $F_\alpha=\bigoplus_{\beta<\alpha}Se_\beta$, $L_\alpha=L\cap F_\alpha$.  Then
$$
L_{\alpha+1}/L_\alpha
$$
embeds, via projection to the $\alpha$-th coordinate, into $S$; hence it is isomorphic to an ideal of $S$, and is projective.  Therefore
$$
0\to L_\alpha\to L_{\alpha+1}\to L_{\alpha+1}/L_\alpha\to 0
$$
splits.  At limit ordinals, $L_\lambda=\bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}L_\alpha$, because elements of a direct sum have finite support.  By transfinite induction, $L$ is a direct sum of projective modules isomorphic to the successive quotients, hence is projective.  Thus the kernel of a free presentation of any module is projective, so $\operatorname{gl.dim}S\le1$.

**Fact 2.** In a commutative von Neumann regular ring, every countably generated ideal is projective.

Recall first that every finitely generated ideal is generated by an idempotent.  If $I=(a_1,\dots,a_m)$, choose $b_i$ with $a_i=a_i^2b_i$, and put $e_i=a_i b_i$.  Then $e_i^2=e_i$ and $Sa_i=Se_i$.  Hence
$$
I=S e,\qquad e=1-\prod_{i=1}^m(1-e_i).
$$
Now let $I=(a_1,a_2,\dots)$, and set $I_n=(a_1,\dots,a_n)$, $I_0=0$.  Choose an idempotent $p_n$ with $I_n=Sp_n$.  Since $I_{n-1}\subseteq I_n$, we have $p_{n-1}p_n=p_{n-1}$.  Define
$$
f_1=p_1,\qquad f_n=p_n(1-p_{n-1})\quad(n\ge2).
$$
The $f_n$'s are pairwise orthogonal idempotents and
$$
I_n=I_{n-1}\oplus S f_n.
$$
Thus $I=\bigoplus_{n\ge1}Sf_n$, so $I$ is projective.  In particular, if the ring is countable, then every ideal is countably generated, hence projective.

## 1. Admissibility

Both $A$ and $B$ have characteristic $3$, since both contain $K=\mathbb F_3$ as their subring of constant coefficients.  Therefore $2\cdot 1$ is a unit in both rings: in $\mathbb F_3$, $2^{-1}=2$.

The identity element of $G$ has order $1$, and every nonidentity element has order $2$.  Therefore the admissibility condition requires only that $1$ and $2$ be units, which we have just checked.  Hence both $(A,G)$ and $(B,G)$ are admissible.

## 2. The coefficient rings both have global dimension $1$

### The ring $A=K[G]$

For $m\ge0$, let
$$
H_m=\langle g_0,\dots,g_m\rangle\cong C_2^{m+1}.
$$
Every element of $A$ lies in some $K[H_m]$, so $A=\bigcup_{m\ge0}K[H_m]$.  Each $K[H_m]$ is finite, and hence $A$ is countable.

By Maschke’s theorem: if $H$ is finite and $|H|$ is invertible in a field $K$, then $K[H]$ is semisimple artinian.  Here $|H_m|=2^{m+1}$, and $2$ is invertible in $\mathbb F_3$.  Thus $K[H_m]$ is semisimple; since it is commutative, it is a finite product of fields.  (Explicitly, $K[C_2]\cong K\times K$, because $T^2-1=(T-1)(T+1)$ over $\mathbb F_3$, and then one takes tensor products.)  Consequently, if $a\in A$, then $a\in K[H_m]$ for some $m$, and inside the product of fields $K[H_m]$ there is $b\in K[H_m]$ with $a=a^2b$.  Hence $A$ is von Neumann regular.

Since $A$ is countable, Fact 2 shows that every ideal of $A$ is projective.  Fact 1 then gives
$$
\operatorname{gl.dim}A\le1.
$$

It remains to see that $\operatorname{gl.dim}A\ne0$.  In $K$, $2$ is invertible, so the following elements are defined:
$$
u_i^+:=\frac{1+g_i}{2},\qquad u_i^-:=\frac{1-g_i}{2}.
$$
They are complementary idempotents: $(u_i^\pm)^2=u_i^\pm$ and $u_i^+u_i^-=0$.  Define
$$
f_n:=u_n^-\prod_{i<n}u_i^+\qquad(n\ge0).
$$
Then each $f_n$ is idempotent.  For $m<n$, the product $f_mf_n$ contains both $u_m^-$ and $u_m^+$, so $f_mf_n=0$.  Thus the $f_n$'s are pairwise orthogonal.

Also $f_n\ne0$.  To see this, restrict to $K[H_n]$.  The map
$$
K[H_n]\longrightarrow \prod_{\varepsilon\in\{\pm1\}^{n+1}}K,\qquad
g_i\mapsto (\varepsilon_i)_\varepsilon
$$
is an isomorphism.  Under this map, $f_n$ has coordinate $1$ at the sign vector $\varepsilon_0=\cdots=\varepsilon_{n-1}=1$, $\varepsilon_n=-1$, and coordinate $0$ elsewhere.  Hence $f_n\ne0$.

Let
$$
J:=\sum_{n\ge0}Af_n.
$$
The ideal $J$ is not a direct summand of $A$.  Indeed, if $J$ were a direct summand, then, since $A$ is commutative, $J=Ae$ for some idempotent $e$.  But $e\in J$, so $e$ is a finite sum $e=\sum_{i\in F}a_i f_i$.  Choose $m\notin F$.  Since $f_m\in J=Ae$, we get $f_m=ef_m$.  But $ef_m=0$, because every $f_i$ occurring in $e$ is orthogonal to $f_m$.  This contradicts $f_m\ne0$.

Therefore the cyclic module $A/J$ is not projective: if it were projective, then $0\to J\to A\to A/J\to0$ would split and $J$ would be a direct summand.  Hence $\operatorname{gl.dim}A\ge1$.  Together with $\operatorname{gl.dim}A\le1$, this gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{gl.dim}A=1.}
$$

### The ring $B=K[X]$

The polynomial ring $K[X]$ is a PID.  Hence every ideal is principal, and every nonzero principal ideal is isomorphic to $B$ as a $B$-module; the zero ideal is projective.  Thus every ideal is projective, and Fact 1 gives $\operatorname{gl.dim}B\le1$.

The module $B/(X)$ is not projective.  Otherwise $(X)$ would be a direct summand of $B$, hence $(X)=Be$ for an idempotent $e$.  But $K[X]$ is a domain, so its only idempotents are $0$ and $1$, while $(X)$ is neither $0$ nor $B$.  Thus $\operatorname{gl.dim}B\ge1$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{gl.dim}B=1.}
$$

So
$$
\operatorname{gl.dim}R_1=\operatorname{gl.dim}R_2=1.
$$

## 3. The group ring over $R_2$ has global dimension $2$

We first compute $\operatorname{gl.dim}A[X]$.  Let $S=A[X]$, and let $M=A/J$, viewed as an $S$-module with $X$ acting as $0$.  From Section 2, $M$ is not $A$-projective and $\operatorname{gl.dim}A=1$, so $\operatorname{pd}_A M=1$.

### Upper bound $\operatorname{gl.dim}S\le2$

Let $N$ be any $S$-module.  Write the action of $X$ on $N$ as $n\mapsto Xn$.  There is an exact sequence of $S$-modules
$$
0\longrightarrow S\otimes_A N
\xrightarrow{\delta}
S\otimes_A N
\xrightarrow{\epsilon}
N
\longrightarrow 0,
$$
where
$$
\delta(X^i\otimes n)=X^{i+1}\otimes n-X^i\otimes Xn,
\qquad
\epsilon(X^i\otimes n)=X^i n.
$$
Exactness is immediate from the direct-sum decomposition $S\otimes_A N=\bigoplus_{i\ge0}X^i\otimes N$: $\delta$ is injective by looking at the term of highest $X$-degree, and its cokernel is identified with $N$ because the relations imposed by $\delta$ give $X^{i+1}\otimes n\equiv X^i\otimes Xn$.

Since $\operatorname{gl.dim}A=1$, the $A$-module $N$ has projective dimension at most $1$.  As $S$ is flat over $A$, induction from $A$ to $S$ shows
$$
\operatorname{pd}_S(S\otimes_A N)\le1.
$$
The standard projective-dimension inequality in a short exact sequence $0\to U\to V\to W\to0$,
$$
\operatorname{pd}W\le\max\{\operatorname{pd}V,\operatorname{pd}U+1\},
$$
then gives $\operatorname{pd}_S N\le2$.  Hence
$$
\operatorname{gl.dim}S\le2.
$$

### Lower bound $\operatorname{gl.dim}S\ge2$

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that $\operatorname{pd}_S M\le1$.  Choose an exact sequence of $S$-modules
$$
0\to Q\to P\to M\to0
$$
with $P$ and $Q$ projective over $S$.  Since $XM=0$, we have $XP\subseteq Q$.

Because $P$ is $S$-projective, it is a direct summand of a free $S$-module.  Multiplication by $X$ is injective on every free $S$-module, hence also on $P$; similarly it is injective on $Q$.  Moreover $P/XP$ and $Q/XQ$ are projective as $A=S/(X)$-modules.

Reducing the presentation modulo $X$ gives an exact sequence of $A$-modules
$$
0\to Q/XP\to P/XP\to M\to0.
$$
Here $P/XP$ is $A$-projective.  Since $\operatorname{pd}_A M\le1$, every first syzygy of $M$ is projective; equivalently, by Schanuel’s lemma, $Q/XP$ is $A$-projective.

Now consider
$$
0\to XP/XQ\to Q/XQ\to Q/XP\to0.
$$
Its kernel is $XP/XQ$ because $XP\subseteq Q$.  Multiplication by $X$ induces an isomorphism
$$
P/Q\cong XP/XQ,
$$
since multiplication by $X$ is injective on $P$ and carries $Q$ isomorphically onto $XQ$.  As $P/Q\cong M$, we obtain an exact sequence of $A$-modules
$$
0\to M\to Q/XQ\to Q/XP\to0.
$$
Both $Q/XQ$ and $Q/XP$ are $A$-projective.  Since the quotient $Q/XP$ is projective, this sequence splits, so $M$ is a direct summand of the projective $A$-module $Q/XQ$.  Thus $M$ would be $A$-projective, contrary to its construction.

Therefore $\operatorname{pd}_S M>1$.  Combined with the upper bound, this shows
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{gl.dim}A[X]=2.}
$$

Now $R_2[G]=K[X][G]$.  There is a natural $K$-algebra isomorphism
$$
K[X][G]\cong K[G][X]=A[X],
$$
given by
$$
\sum_g\Bigl(\sum_i a_{i,g}X^i\Bigr)[g]
\longmapsto
\sum_i\Bigl(\sum_g a_{i,g}[g]\Bigr)X^i.
$$
Multiplication is preserved because $X$ and the group elements commute on both sides.  Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{gl.dim}R_2[G]=2.}
$$

## 4. The group ring over $R_1$ has global dimension $1$

For any abelian groups $H,L$, there is a natural isomorphism
$$
K[H][L]\cong K[H\oplus L],
\qquad
\left(\sum_h k_h[h]\right)[\ell]\mapsto \sum_h k_h[(h,\ell)].
$$
Therefore
$$
R_1[G]=K[G][G]\cong K[G\oplus G].
$$
Since $G=\bigoplus_{\mathbb N}C_2$, the group $G\oplus G$ is again a direct sum of countably many copies of $C_2$; explicitly, use the bijection
$$
\mathbb N\sqcup\mathbb N\cong\mathbb N
$$
to identify the indexing sets.  Hence $G\oplus G\cong G$, and so
$$
R_1[G]\cong K[G]=R_1.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{gl.dim}R_1[G]=1.}
$$

## Conclusion

We have exhibited
$$
G=\bigoplus_{\mathbb N}C_2,\qquad
R_1=\mathbb F_3[G],\qquad
R_2=\mathbb F_3[X].
$$
Both pairs $(R_1,G)$ and $(R_2,G)$ are admissible: the only finite orders in $G$ are $1$ and $2$, and $1,2$ are units in both characteristic $3$ rings.

Moreover
$$
\operatorname{gl.dim}R_1=\operatorname{gl.dim}R_2=1,
$$
while
$$
\operatorname{gl.dim}R_1[G]=1
\quad\text{and}\quad
\operatorname{gl.dim}R_2[G]=2.
$$
Thus the proposed implication is false, even for one fixed abelian group $G$ satisfying the admissibility condition for both coefficient rings.
