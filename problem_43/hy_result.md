# Solution to Problem 43

## The relaxation

I weaken hypothesis (5) as follows.

> **(5′)** For every height-one prime ideal $Q$ of $\overline S$, the local ring
> $R_{Q\cap R}$ is **normal**.

Hypotheses (1)–(4) are kept unchanged. The original hypothesis (5) says that these local rings are regular, and every regular local Noetherian ring is normal; hence (5) implies (5′). The weakening is strict: in the example below, one such local ring is normal but not regular.

We shall use the following standard theorems:

* Serre’s criterion: a Noetherian domain is normal iff it satisfies $R_1$ and $S_2$.
* A hypersurface over a field is Cohen–Macaulay; polynomial extensions and localizations of Cohen–Macaulay rings are Cohen–Macaulay.
* Fields are excellent, excellence is stable under finitely generated algebras and localizations, and if $A$ is excellent then for every $r$ the $S_r$-locus of every algebra essentially of finite type over $A$ is open. This is Grothendieck’s openness theorem for the $S_r$ condition.

## The example

Let $k=\mathbb Q$, and set

$$
R=k[U,V,W]/(UV-W^2).
$$

Write $u,v,w$ for the images of $U,V,W$. The polynomial $UV-W^2$ is irreducible: as a polynomial in $V$ it has coprime coefficients $U$ and $-W^2$ in the UFD $k[U,W]$, hence it is irreducible by Gauss’s lemma. Since $k[U,V,W]$ is a UFD, it is prime. Thus $R$ is a Noetherian domain.

Let $K=\operatorname{Frac}(R)$, and put

$$
t=\frac wu\in K.
$$

Then

$$
w=ut,\qquad v=ut^2.
$$

Also $R_u\cong k[u,u^{-1},w]$, so $K=k(u,w)=k(u,t)$. Define

$$
B:=R[t]=k[u,t]\subset K,
\qquad
S:=R[t^2]=k[u,t^2,ut]\subset B.
$$

Here $B$ really is the polynomial ring $k[u,t]$: it contains $u$ and $t$, while $w=ut$ and $v=ut^2$, so $R[t]\subseteq k[u,t]$, and the reverse inclusion is immediate. Also $S$ contains $u$ and $ut$, so $\operatorname{Frac}(S)=K$. Let $W=R\setminus\{0\}$. Since $R\subseteq S\subseteq K$,

$$
L=K\otimes_R S\cong W^{-1}S.
$$

This localization embeds in $K$. Conversely every $a/b\in K$, with $a,b\in R$ and $b\ne0$, lies in $W^{-1}S$, because $a\in S$ and $b$ is inverted there. Hence $L=K$. We shall identify $L$ with $K$ from now on and show that $B=\overline S$. First note some useful presentations.

### Two presentations

As an $R$-algebra,

$$
S\cong R[Y]/(uY-v),\qquad Y\mapsto t^2.
$$

Indeed, after imposing $uY=v$, the relation $uv-w^2$ becomes $u^2Y-w^2$, so the quotient by $(uY-v)$ is

$$
\mathcal Q=k[u,w,Y]/(w^2-u^2Y).
$$

Its natural map to $S$, $u\mapsto u$, $w\mapsto ut$, $Y\mapsto t^2$, is surjective. To see that it is injective, write any element of $\mathcal Q$ uniquely as $P(u,Y)+wQ(u,Y)$ with $P,Q\in k[u,Y]$. Its image in $k[u,t]$ is

$$
P(u,t^2)+ut\,Q(u,t^2).
$$

The first summand contains only monomials with even $t$-degree, and the second only monomials with odd $t$-degree. Hence both summands vanish. Since $u,Y$ are indeterminates and the substitution $Y\mapsto t^2$ is injective on $k[u,Y]$, $P=Q=0$. Thus the displayed presentation of $S$ is correct.

Similarly,

$$
B\cong R[Z]/(uZ-w,\ v-uZ^2),\qquad Z\mapsto t.
$$

Eliminating $w$ and $v$ gives $k[u,Z]$, mapping isomorphically to $k[u,t]$ by $u\mapsto u$, $Z\mapsto t$; the relation $uv-w^2$ is then automatically satisfied.

### $S$ and its normalization

Put $A=k[u,t^2]$. Since $(ut)^2=u^2t^2\in A$,

$$
S=A+utA.
$$

This sum is direct: $A$ consists of monomials with even $t$-degree, while $utA$ consists of monomials with odd $t$-degree. Consequently $t\notin S$: if $t=ut\,a(u,t^2)$, then cancelling the nonzero element $t$ in the domain $k[u,t]$ would give $1=u\,a(u,t^2)$, impossible in $k[u,t^2]$.

Also

$$
B=k[u,t]=k[u,t^2]+t\,k[u,t^2]=S+tS,
$$

so $B$ is generated by $1,t$ as an $S$-module. Since $t^2\in S$, $t$ is integral over $S$, and therefore $B$ is integral and module-finite over $S$. The ring $B=k[u,t]$ is a polynomial ring over a field, hence normal. As $B\supset S$ and $B$ is normal in the same fraction field $K$, it follows that

$$
\overline S=B.
$$

Indeed, every element of $B$ is integral over $S$, while any element integral over $S$ is also integral over $B$, hence lies in $B$. Thus hypothesis (4) is satisfied.

## Verification of the retained hypotheses

### Hypothesis (1)

The field $k=\mathbb Q$ is excellent. Since $R$ is finitely generated over $k$, $R$ is excellent. By Grothendieck’s openness theorem for the $S_r$ condition, every algebra essentially of finite type over $R$ has open $S_2$-locus. Thus (1) holds.

### Hypothesis (2)

From the presentation $S\cong R[Y]/(uY-v)$, $S$ is essentially of finite type over $R$. Since $R\subset S\subset K$, $S$ is torsion-free over $R$. We already identified $L\cong K$. Therefore $K\otimes_R S$ is the single field $K$, which is a finite separable extension of itself. Thus $S$ is generically étale over $R$, and (2) holds.

### Basic properties of $R$

We record that $R$ is normal. The ring $R$ is a hypersurface, hence Cohen–Macaulay, so it satisfies $S_2$. Let $\mathfrak p$ be a height-one prime of $R$. If $u\notin\mathfrak p$, then

$$
R_{\mathfrak p}
$$

is a localization of

$$
R_u\cong k[u,u^{-1},w],
$$

so it is regular. If $v\notin\mathfrak p$, then it is a localization of

$$
R_v\cong k[v,v^{-1},w],
$$

again regular. A height-one prime cannot contain both $u$ and $v$: if it did, then $w^2=uv\in\mathfrak p$, hence $w\in\mathfrak p$, so $\mathfrak p$ would contain

$$
\mathfrak m=(u,v,w).
$$

But $\mathfrak m$ has height $2$. First, $\dim R=2$: the quotient of $k[U,V,W]$ by the nonzero prime $(UV-W^2)$ has dimension at most $2$, while

$$
(0)\subset (u,w)\subset (u,v,w)=\mathfrak m
$$

is a chain of primes because $R/(u,w)\cong k[v]$; hence $\dim R\ge2$. The same chain gives $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak m\ge2$, and the reverse inequality follows from $\dim R=2$. Thus no height-one prime contains both $u$ and $v$. Therefore $R$ is regular at all height-one primes. By Serre’s criterion, $R$ is normal.

### Hypothesis (3)

Let $M$ be a maximal ideal of $S$, let $P=M\cap R$, and let $\mathfrak q$ be the inverse image of $M$ in $R_P[Y]$. Put

$$
T=(R_P[Y])_{\mathfrak q}.
$$

Using $S\cong R[Y]/(uY-v)$, we get

$$
S_M\cong T/I,\qquad I=(uY-v)T.
$$

Since $S$ is a domain, $T/I\cong S_M$ is a domain, so $I$ is a prime ideal. It is generated by one nonzero element in the domain $T$; hence its height is $1$. Thus $I$ has pure height $h=1$.

The ring $R$ is a hypersurface, hence Cohen–Macaulay. Therefore $R_P$, $R_P[Y]$, and its localization $T$ are Cohen–Macaulay. Consequently, for every prime $\mathfrak r\in\operatorname{Spec}(T)$ containing $I$, the localization $T_{\mathfrak r}$ is Cohen–Macaulay, and so

$$
\operatorname{depth}T_{\mathfrak r}
=
\dim T_{\mathfrak r}
=
\operatorname{ht}_T\mathfrak r.
$$

Since $h+2=3$,

$$
\operatorname{depth}T_{\mathfrak r}
=
\operatorname{ht}_T\mathfrak r
\ge
\min\{\operatorname{ht}_T\mathfrak r,h+2\}.
$$

Also $R_P$ is normal because $R$ is normal. Thus (3) holds.

### Hypothesis (4)

We already proved that $\overline S=B=k[u,t]$ and that $B=S+tS$ is module-finite over $S$. Thus (4) holds.

### The weakened hypothesis (5′), and failure of original (5)

Since $R$ is normal, every localization of $R$ is normal. Hence (5′) holds.

However, the original hypothesis (5) fails. In $B=k[u,t]$, the prime

$$
Q=(u)
$$

has height $1$. Its contraction to $R$ is

$$
Q\cap R=(u,v,w)=\mathfrak m,
$$

because $u,v=ut^2,w=ut$ all lie in $uB$, while $\mathfrak m$ is maximal and $1\notin uB$. We have $\dim R_{\mathfrak m}=2$. To see that $R_{\mathfrak m}$ is not regular, localize the presentation

$$
R=k[U,V,W]/(UV-W^2)
$$

at $(U,V,W)$. Since $UV-W^2\in (U,V,W)^2$,

$$
\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2
\cong
(U,V,W)/(U,V,W)^2,
$$

which has $k$-dimension $3$. Thus the maximal ideal of $R_{\mathfrak m}$ cannot be generated by $\dim R_{\mathfrak m}=2$ elements. Therefore $R_{\mathfrak m}$ is not regular.

So the example satisfies all retained hypotheses and (5′), but not original (5).

## Jacobian ideals and failure of the conclusion

Since $\overline S=B$, we compute the two Jacobian ideals from the presentations above.

For $S$, use

$$
S\cong R[Y]/(uY-v).
$$

There is one variable and one equation, whose derivative with respect to $Y$ is $u$. Hence

$$
J_{S/R}=uS.
$$

For $B=\overline S$, use

$$
B\cong R[Z]/(uZ-w,\ v-uZ^2).
$$

There is one variable and two equations. The $1\times1$ minors of the derivative matrix have entries

$$
\frac{\partial(uZ-w)}{\partial Z}=u,\qquad
\frac{\partial(v-uZ^2)}{\partial Z}=-2uZ.
$$

Thus

$$
J_{B/R}=uB.
$$

Here $L=K$, as shown above. Because $u\neq0$ in the field $L$,

$$
(B:_L uB)=u^{-1}B,\qquad
(S:_L uS)=u^{-1}S.
$$

Indeed, $z(uE)\subseteq E$ for $E=B$ or $E=S$ holds iff $zu\in E$, since $1\in E$.

Now take

$$
z=\frac tu\in L.
$$

Then

$$
z\,uB=tB\subseteq B,
$$

so

$$
z\in(B:_L J_{B/R})=(\overline S:_L J_{\overline S/R}).
$$

On the other hand, $u\in J_{S/R}=uS$, and

$$
z\,u=t\notin S.
$$

Therefore $zJ_{S/R}\not\subseteq S$, so

$$
z\notin(S:_L J_{S/R}).
$$

Thus

$$
\frac tu\in
(\overline S:_L J_{\overline S/R})
\setminus
(S:_L J_{S/R}),
$$

and the displayed inclusion fails. This gives the required counterexample to the weakened theorem.
